Why fighting fair isn't good enough

Learning to crash gracefully is not the same as learning to fly.

And above all, to God, who never once taught us how to fight better. Only how to stop.

From the acknowledgments

Nearly every book, seminar, and counseling session says the same thing: conflict is normal, so learn to manage it. Stay calm. Listen actively. Don't attack the person. It's decent advice, and it beats shouting matches and cold wars. But underneath it sits an assumption almost nobody questions: that conflict, even handled well, has to stay a permanent feature of your marriage.

That's like telling a pilot to crash more gracefully instead of teaching them to fly higher and avoid the crash altogether.

Conflict and fighting are not the same thing. Conflict is two people who see something differently. Fighting is what happens when one or both of them arrive unprepared. The difference isn't temperament or luck — it's preparation. And preparation can be learned.

Read more about the plan →

The plan

Two people. Two separate preparations. One conversation that finally goes somewhere.

Most couples walk into a hard conversation the moment one of them can't hold it in any longer. Both arrive unready — one carrying pain, the other bracing for impact. This book gives each spouse their own path to walk first.

For the one who was hurt

The Safety Plan

"How do I say this without being re-wounded?"

  1. Sit still before you speak
  2. Quiet the urge to react
  3. Get emotionally honest
  4. Surrender the need to be right
  5. Choose your presence
  6. Seek counsel
  7. Wait

For the one who caused it

The Readiness Plan

"How do I become someone this is safe to say to?"

  1. Pray first
  2. Quiet the urge to react
  3. Get emotionally honest
  4. Surrender the need to be right
  5. Prepare your presence
  6. Steady your heart
  7. Wait until you're ready

Where they meet

The Anti-Fight Plan

When both people arrive safe, surrendered, and Spirit-led, the goal stops being to win and starts being to become one. Fighting doesn't get managed. It becomes irrelevant.

Let's start with the objection

"That's not realistic."
"Every couple argues."
"You can't avoid conflict forever."

Never fighting again doesn't mean what you think it means.

It doesn't mean you'll never be upset, never disagree, or always feel close. It means disagreement stops turning into disconnection. Specifically:

  • You will not use emotional weapons.
  • You will not talk over each other.
  • You will not yell, shut down, guilt-trip, shame, or withdraw to punish.
  • You will not make it about your rights instead of God's righteousness.

Disagreements don't have to be detonations. They can be doorways.

How it's built

Three parts, in the order you actually need them.

Part One
The foundation

Renewing your mind about marriage and communication. The real difference between conflict and fighting. The call to maturity, and what it means to invite God into the middle of a disagreement rather than after it.

Part Two
The plan

The Safety Plan for the spouse carrying the hurt. The Readiness Plan for the spouse who caused it. Then the Anti-Fight Plan, where the two paths meet and the conversation actually happens.

Part Three
The life after

Forgiveness and fresh starts. What to do when old habits try to return. Becoming a safe place for each other, and leaving a legacy of peace that outlasts you.

The tools

Named frameworks you can use at 10pm on a Tuesday.

Not theory. Things you and your spouse can call by name, in the moment, when it matters.

ACTS
The heart posture

Adoration, Confession, Thanksgiving, and Sharing truth in love — the gospel's own order for entering a hard conversation. Own what's yours before you name what isn't.

LUV
How to respond

Listen, Understand, Validate. When your spouse finishes telling you something hard, they aren't looking for resolution yet. They're looking for recognition.

Stop Loss
The agreed pause

Borrowed from investing: exit early to prevent deeper loss. A pre-agreed cue, a fixed pause, and a real plan to come back — so pausing never becomes walking away.

3D Communication
Words, tone, body

"Don't worry about how I'm saying it" isn't how safety works. Bringing all three dimensions into alignment, so your face doesn't contradict your sentence.

Drive-Through
Reflect it back

The same way a drive-thru repeats your order before it moves on. Say back what you heard, in their words, before you offer anything of your own.

Discussion Guide
Chapter by chapter

Questions for reading alone, as a couple, or in a small group. Slowly is fine — you don't have to answer them all in one sitting.

About the authors

Larry & Rolanda Beacham

A husband-and-wife team devoted to helping couples move from conflict to real connection — starting with the years they spent learning it the hard way themselves.

Their story

Fighting fair still wasn't the same as not fighting.

They did what the books said. And they still kept ending up on opposite sides of the same room.

Larry and Rolanda learned that the long way. They stayed calm, listened actively, kept it clean — everything the advice told them to do.

What eventually changed things wasn't a better technique. It was realizing that both of them were walking into hard conversations unprepared — one carrying pain with nowhere safe to put it, the other bracing for impact and reaching for a defense. Out of those years came the Safety Plan and Anti-Fight Plan framework at the center of this book.

It has since shaped how they lead their own marriage and how they walk alongside other couples. They live in Odessa, Florida, and are parents to Taylor, Bryant, Lauryn, Manny, Ari, and Trey. Never Fight or Argue Again is their first book.

In their words

And above all, to God, who never once taught us how to fight better. Only how to stop.

From the acknowledgments

They're quick to name the people who got them here: mentors, pastors, and counselors who told them the truth they needed instead of the thing they wanted to hear. Chris and Vanessa Davis, who laid a foundation and taught them what resolving conflict in a godly way actually looks like. Pastor Ralph Johnson of Grace Family Church in Tampa, whose wisdom carried them through some of their toughest moments. And their six children, who lived through the years their parents were still figuring it out.
